01 · Meta design brief

Say When

Meta's brief, "Where Plans Go to Die," asked how a messaging app could help friend groups move from "we should hang out" to actually following through. My team designed Say When.

BriefMeta
CourseINFO-I 441, Fall 2026
ProductMessaging feature
Team5 designers
My roleResearch, Figma, prototyping, presenting

Problem

Plans die from uncertainty

The brief was clear that this was never a logistics problem. Our research pointed to something social: plans stall when they're under-detailed, and nobody wants to be the one who pushes. We narrowed our audience to close friends in small group chats.

I personally interviewed 3 people for this project.

Concept

A plan gets its own space

Say When opens a contained planning space from a calendar button in the chat header, next to FaceTime. It holds the whole group. When, where, and how long each get their own row, and each can be proposed, countered, and agreed on without waiting on the others. When the plan has passed, the space closes on its own.

Screens

One plan, start to finish

Open the interactive Figma prototype ↗

Decisions

What we chose and why

D1

Prompts ask about details, not attendance

Prompts focus on time, place, and how long, not on who's going.

WhyUnder-detailed plans were the problem we found, so the prompts go straight at the missing details.

D2

Nothing posts to the main chat

Everything stays inside the planning space, and the microcopy says so.

WhyPlanning shouldn't flood the group chat for people who aren't deciding yet.

D3

Disagreeing adds an option

"Suggest another" lets someone propose an alternative instead of rejecting the plan. Prompts never fill in answers for people: someone proposes, and the group settles it. Reopening an agreed detail creates a new proposal, never a silent change.

D4

Nudge the group once, then stop

When a detail stalls, Say When shows the group two ways forward: ask again once, or change the plan, such as switching to somewhere walkable. It's shown once, to the whole group, and never points at one person.

WhyA reminder that keeps coming back, or calls someone out, feels like nagging and makes people mute the chat.

D5

We cut a feature that pressured people to opt in

An early idea asked friends who hadn't opted in to turn on plan detection in the middle of a conversation. We dropped it.

WhyAsking for consent in front of your friends is social pressure, not real consent. It read as a dark pattern and a privacy step backward.

Outcome

What reviewers said

"The tightest logic chain, the strongest screens, and the most honest deck in the class."

Meta reviewer, Project 1 feedback

The same review pushed us on what to fix, and I'm carrying it into the next project:

What I'd change

  • Name one problem. Our framing let uncertainty, collaboration, and chat structure compete.
  • Start with the simplest version. A threaded reply might do what a separate space does.
  • Show the research. Put how many people we talked to next to every claim.

02 · Twitch design brief

Hype Voyage

Twitch asked us to reimagine the Hype Train on mobile. Our team turned it into a voyage: a ship that sails around the stream as the chat supports the streamer together.

BriefTwitch
CourseINFO-I 441, Fall 2026
ProductLive-stream mobile feature
My roleResearch, Figma screens, prototyping, presenting
Team5 designers
ResultHonorable mention from Twitch

Research

What viewers told us

Twitch told us new users are mostly on mobile, with shorter sessions, and that any new version still has to fit with paid support like Bits and subs without cluttering the screen. To hear from viewers, our team posted two threads on Reddit asking what people like and dislike about Hype Trains.

"you're just throwing bits at a progress bar"

Reddit respondent, our team's thread

Others described the rotating emotes as FOMO and said they hide Hype Trains completely. Some streamers said they wanted more control over the train.

Two findings shaped the design. Viewers often didn't know what rewards were on offer, which we called reward awareness. And viewers who don't spend felt the experience wasn't for them, so pride and a sense of mattering became a goal. We set three principles for the design: excitement, meaning, and cohesion.

Concept

A voyage instead of a progress bar

The ship sails a loop around the edge of the stream past four stops, each revealing a reward the streamer set, toward a destination like a personal-best attempt. Bits, subs, and gifts move the ship. Free actions build morale, which never moves the ship on its own but makes every paid contribution move it further. Viewers who stay aboard collect passport stamps, and everyone aboard at arrival gets a postcard with a captured moment from the stream.

If the ship doesn't make it, it drops anchor at the last stop for a layover, and the next voyage starts from there. A Versus mode lets two streamers race their ships.

Screens

The voyage on mobile

My part

What I did

Hype Voyage started as a teammate's concept, and the team chose it as our lead direction. I contributed research and Figma screens, and worked out the screens and interaction details most likely to cause problems. I used Swift and React to prototype parts of the experience, and I presented a core section of our pitch.

I pushed for the experience to be easy to minimize or hide, and built for both landscape and portrait.

Outcome

Honorable mention

Stephen Hicks, Principal UX Researcher at Twitch, reviewed five teams' concepts and gave Hype Voyage an honorable mention.

"The best free-to-paid loop in the room."

Stephen Hicks, Twitch, final critique

He named morale the strongest free-to-paid mechanic, called the layover the best recovery path, and said we made the clearest connection to Twitch's Q4 business goals of any team.

What I'd change

  • Make progress, the destination, and real rewards obvious on a phone. A thin line around the video and placeholder rewards hid a strong system.
  • Lead with a voyage the streamer starts on purpose, so it never launches at the wrong moment.
  • Simplify. Ports, stops, Studio, Versus, and Fleets added up to a lot for creators to set up.
  • Show the research clearly: what we wanted to learn, who we talked to, and how many. And keep the pride insight in the final story.

03 · INFO-I 300 · HCI

MajorPath

Choosing a major is easier when you can talk to people who've been there. MajorPath helps college students find majors that fit them, then connects them with counselors and alumni who match their interests and goals.

CourseINFO-I 300, Spring 2025
ProductiOS app prototype
Team4 designers, one flow each
My partConnecting with counselors and alumni
ResultOutstanding Project Award

The app

Four flows, four owners

The team split MajorPath into four tasks and each of us owned one: creating an account and taking a short survey; connecting with academic advisors and finding alumni; uploading SAT or ACT scores and taking an aptitude test; and adding transcripts to get major suggestions.

I owned the second one. Once a student has majors in mind, the Connect tab helps them find counselors and alumni to talk to, judge who's worth reaching out to, and get in touch.

Screens

The Connect flow

Decisions

What I designed

D1

Alumni and counselors on one page

The Connect tab puts both groups on one screen with a short preview of each, so a student sees both kinds of help at once and can open the full list when they want more.

D2

Filter by what matters to each group

Alumni filter by major, job field, experience, and rating. Counselors filter by department, experience, and rating.

D3

Ratings and honest reviews

Each profile shows an overall rating, lets the student leave their own, and lists anonymous reviews, including critical ones like "Was hard to reach out to..."

WhyAnonymous reviews let students say what they really think without worrying that their name will be tied to it later.

D4

Contact details behind one tap

A Contact Information button opens email and department details only when a student decides to reach out.

WhyContact details are somewhat personal, so reaching out should be a deliberate choice. Keeping them behind a button also sets a boundary and makes them harder for bots to scrape.

Process

Three rounds of sketches, three of prototypes

We worked through sketch versions 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0, then prototypes 2.5, 3.0, and 3.5, and tested the app with users along the way. My Connections page started as a black-and-white wireframe and ended as a styled screen with real faces and a shared tab bar.

Outcome

Outstanding Project

Our class voted MajorPath the Outstanding Project Award, and the professor gave it a great grade.

Moodwave

A web app that turns a color into a playlist. Pick a color for your mood, add a public Spotify profile if you want, and get 50 songs that blend your mood with your taste.

    Thumbs down removes a song and pulls in the next one.Songs hand-picked to show the layout. The real app finds tracks through Spotify search.

    This demo runs the mood side of Moodwave in your browser with a simplified version of the mapping. The full app also pulls your public Spotify playlists and searches Spotify for real tracks, which needs API keys that can't go on a public page.

    Decisions

    The spec

    D1

    Warm colors mean energy, cool colors mean calm

    Hue sets the kind of mood, saturation sets how intense it is, and lightness sets how positive it feels. Together they become targets for valence, energy, tempo, danceability, and acousticness.

    D2

    Ground the mapping in real data

    I picked the Moodify dataset from Kaggle, about 278,000 songs labeled happy, energetic, calm, or sad. A color that sits between moods gets a blend of their targets instead of snapping to one.

    D3

    Public playlists only, and say so

    Users paste a public Spotify profile link. No login is needed, and the app tells users that only public playlists can be read. People with no playlists get recommendations from mood alone.

    D4

    Export a list instead of writing to Spotify

    The playlist lives in the app as a list of 50 songs that users can rate and export as CSV, JSON, Spotify links, or an M3U file.

    WhyWriting playlists across Spotify, Apple Music, and YouTube runs into their terms of service. A list works anywhere.

    My part

    How it was built

    My team helped shape the decisions. The build was me and Claude: I turned our idea into a full spec, Claude wrote the code, and I set up the Spotify developer app, ran it, and tested it.

    The first working version returned "Spotify unavailable." Spotify had shut down the recommendations endpoint for most developer apps, so we switched to a search-based approach: run several mood-matched searches, fetch each track's audio features, and rank tracks by how closely they match the mood. A small server keeps the API keys out of the browser.
